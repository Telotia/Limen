A wetland held in blue

A palm-sized body turns the weight of an animal into a surface that holds light.

Collection facts

Standing Hippopotamus

Standing Hippopotamus, ancient Egypt, Middle Kingdom, Dynasty 12–13. Faience; length 11.7 cm, height 5.7 cm, width 5 cm. Accession 26.7.898. The collection record does not identify an individual maker. [1]

Visual analysis

Composition | Weight on short legs

Look past the decoration to the silhouette. The back and belly form a horizontal oval, supported close to the ground by short legs. The broad muzzle pulls that compact mass to the right. Small ears interrupt the curve just enough to give it a pulse. The museum photograph takes an oblique view, showing front and flank together: the object’s design and the photograph’s framing both shape what we see.

Visual analysis

Colour | Nearby hues, distinct values

The glaze moves between cyan-blue and blue-green: an analogous relationship of neighbouring cool hues. Dark motifs separate from brighter glaze chiefly through value contrast. The neutral grey backdrop reduces competition, making the blue feel concentrated. Earthy patches introduce small warm notes. These belong to the surface we see today; they should not be treated as proof of an ancient colour scheme.

Visual analysis

Light | Where the body turns

Paler areas across the shoulder and muzzle suggest surfaces turning toward the light. Shade under the belly and between the feet gives this small object weight. Uneven gleams beside worn areas keep the blue from reading as a flat fill. These observations concern the museum photograph, not a reconstruction of lighting in an ancient setting.

Material facts and observation

Technique | Blue made in the firing

Egyptian faience has a quartz-rich body and a glassy glaze; copper produces its characteristic turquoise. It differs from ordinary clay pottery. A museum technical essay explains how the body and glaze interact with light, but does not establish this object’s particular glazing method. [2] In the image, dark curves follow changes in volume, making decoration appear to grow around the body.

Collection facts

The maker | An unnamed craft tradition

The maker is unidentified. Its secure context is Middle Kingdom Egyptian craftsmanship. The record notes that this figurine was found within mummy wrappings and describes wetland plants and animals as decoration on related hippopotamus figures. [1] A funerary context does not establish every meaning of this particular object, or supply a biography for its maker.

Editorial interpretation

Why it moves me | Near enough to hold

The roundness catches my attention first; the incomplete blue holds it longer. A broad muzzle, low belly and tiny ears bring a heavy animal within an intimate viewing distance. The dark plant forms seem to let the body contain its own habitat. Knowing that it lay within wrappings gives its smallness a quiet sense of companionship. That is a present-day response, not evidence of an ancient person’s feelings.

Practical exercise

Try it | One mass, a few lines

Build a still-life poster with 70% warm white #F2EFE6, 25% cyan-blue #14818D and 5% deep teal #163C41. These are editorial exercise colours and proportions, not exact samples or measurements of the object. Give the main colour one generous silhouette; let a few dark lines curve with its volume. Reduce the design to a phone thumbnail and check that the subject still reads. For a small object photograph, try soft light from above and to one side, keeping a contact shadow beneath the feet.
