A Fan Drifts Through Time

Fans upon Waves · Unidentified Japanese artist · Mid-17th century · Six-panel screen

The water carries a rhythm. The fans keep their own pauses.

Let your eye drift

Follow the pale wave band along the lower edge, then return to the fans. The current links them; their spokes turn your gaze in competing directions. The pleasure of this screen lies in moving between those two speeds.

Composition | A line with pauses

The wide format turns looking into a passage through time. Low curves hold the composition together, while the fans create uneven stops: clustered at the left, more dispersed elsewhere. The broad gold area above the centre gives the eye room to linger. Vertical panel divisions mark a beat that the waves flow across. Empty space has visual weight here.

Colour | Warm ground, cool interruption

Gold, ochre and brown form a muted warm analogous field. Pale wave bands lift it through value contrast. Blue-green and small green accents introduce a cooler register. The blue-green against orange-gold can be read as a muted near-complementary relationship; green and gold are not strict complements. Surrounding warmth makes the small cool passages appear more distinct through simultaneous contrast. A suggested palette: paper #F3EBDD, aged gold #B48B55, ink brown #493E32 and blue-grey #527A7B. These are interpretive screen-based choices, not measurements of the object.

Light | Painted and encountered

Overlaps and modest tonal changes suggest the fans’ volume. The waves rely more on alternating pale and dark bands than on illusionistic shadows. Gold leaf also responds to actual illumination and the angle of a folded screen. A photograph fixes one appearance of that material. Imagine stepping sideways: some of the light belongs to the room.

Technique | Fine lines on gold

The collection record lists ink, colour and gold leaf on paper. Radiating fan spokes feel short and dense; the wave contours feel long and continuous. Each fan surface offers another small field of imagery. Visible squares and mottling articulate the gold ground, but the photograph alone cannot establish the making or ageing process behind every mark, or identify individual pigments.

Artist | Leave the name open

The museum records no named maker, so the attribution remains an unidentified Japanese artist. This is an Edo-period work. Its discussion connects the floating-fan motif with a visual tradition associated with Rinpa; that context does not establish a particular artist or lineage.

Why it moves | Light things, lasting ground

For me, the screen holds a tension between intimacy and distance. Fans belong near hands and bodies, yet here they travel through an unpeopled space. The gold ground gives their passing movement an enduring surface. A small object seems to remember contact without showing its owner. That is a reading of the image, not a claim about the maker’s intent.

Try it | Give repetition room

For a landscape poster, try roughly 55% paper, 30% aged gold, 10% ink brown and 5% blue-grey. Connect three to five fan-shaped modules with one curve; vary their angles and spacing, leaving one conspicuous pause. Place the cool accent in a single module. Reduce the layout to phone width and check that the curve still guides the eye and the title remains clear. This is a design exercise, not the screen’s measured colour distribution.
